The Quiet Revolution of Small Tools

For a decade the software industry chased platforms: vast, integrated surfaces that promised to do everything and ended up mediating everything. The counter-movement arrived quietly, one small tool at a time, each doing exactly one job and getting out of the way.

The economics are simple. A tool that greps, a tool that formats, a tool that extracts the main text of a web page — each is cheap to audit, cheap to replace, and composes with the rest through plain text. Platforms, by contrast, accumulate switching costs until leaving them costs more than staying.

What changed was distribution. Package managers made a single-binary tool installable in a second, and repositories gave it a changelog, an issue tracker and a test suite. The unix philosophy did not need to be argued for; it only needed to be packaged well.

The best tool is the one you forget you are using.

Extraction is a case in point. Pulling the article out of a page of chrome, advertising and recommendation widgets used to require a stack of parsers and heuristics glued together in Python. The same heuristics now compile into a single native function, and the glue disappears.

None of this is nostalgia for pipes and filters. It is an observation about maintenance: small tools age well because their surface is small enough for one person to hold, and one person is usually all they get.
